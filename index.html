Section 01 · National Ten-Year Trajectory

Proactive prevention surged by 39%, cutting emergency rescues even as unpatrolled drownings reached a 20-year high.

Over the past decade (2014/15–2023/24), Australian surf life saving services transformed how they protect the coast. Rather than waiting for swimmers to be swept into rips, patrolling members stepped up preventative actions—warning beachgoers, moving hazards, and shepherd-guiding swimmers back between the flags—from 1.20 million in 2014/15 to 2,482,013 in 2023/24. As proactive warnings climbed 39% above the ten-year average, emergency in-water rescues fell 13% to 8,857.

Yet coastal drowning deaths moved in the opposite direction. Since the post-lockdown domestic coastal tourism boom of 2020/21, annual fatalities have stayed consistently above the ten-year mean of 128 deaths per year, peaking at 150 deaths in 2023/24 (0.56 per 100,000 Australians). Every single fatal incident happened at an unpatrolled location, outside patrol hours, or outside the red and yellow flags.

Section 02 · Geographic Risk & Per-Capita Standardisation

New South Wales recorded the highest raw toll, but Tasmania suffered triple the national drowning rate.

Mapping raw death counts across states would mislead readers by simply reflecting population size: New South Wales (population 8.36 million) recorded 61 coastal drowning deaths, whereas island Tasmania (0.57 million) recorded 10 deaths. Standardising by Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) resident population reveals the true per-capita risk: Tasmania’s coastal drowning rate of 1.74 per 100,000 people is more than three times the national rate of 0.56 and more than double New South Wales (0.73) and Western Australia (0.69).

The year-on-year slope comparison shows why smaller jurisdictions experience sharp rate swings: Tasmania’s toll rose from 3 deaths in 2022/23 to 10 in 2023/24, driving its rate from 0.52 to 1.74. Meanwhile, Queensland reduced its coastal toll from 27 to 23 deaths (0.49 to 0.42 per 100,000), staying well below the national benchmark alongside Victoria (0.38) and South Australia (0.48).

Section 03 · Patrol Capacity, Cartogram Distortion & Remoteness

Volunteer patrols cluster heavily along the east coast, while 69% of deaths happen more than a kilometre away.

Combining SLSA patrol records with ABS and Geoscience Australia coastline data highlights a stark structural divide. Nearly three-quarters of Australia’s 1,462,533 volunteer patrol hours and 45,236 active patrolling members are concentrated in New South Wales (685,090 hours across 129 clubs) and Queensland (356,662 hours across 57 clubs). In the non-contiguous cartogram below, where each state’s land area is scaled in proportion to its patrolling members, the eastern states dominate the map while Tasmania (792 patrollers across 15 clubs) shrinks to a tiny dark-red island.

Because surf life saving clubs can only guard a fraction of Australia’s vast coastline, 104 of the 150 coastal drowning deaths (69%) occurred more than 1 km from the nearest club or lifeguard service. In Tasmania, where rugged coast stretches 2,833 km with only 5.3 clubs per 1,000 km, 90% of drowning deaths (9 of 10) happened beyond the 1 km radius.

Section 04 · Locations, Activities & Demographic Disparity

Beaches, swimming, and men account for the overwhelming majority of coastal drowning deaths.

Where and how do fatal incidents unfold? More than half of all coastal drowning deaths occurred at a beach (56.0%, 84 of 150 deaths), followed by offshore waters (18.7%, 28), rocky foreshores or cliffs (12.7%, 19), and other coastal waters (12.7%, 19). Among the 84 beach fatalities, 29 (34.5%) were confirmed in a rip current, 20 (23.8%) did not involve a rip, and 35 (41.7%) had no witness record to confirm or rule out a rip. By activity, swimming and wading jumped to 40.7% (61 deaths)—up sharply from its ten-year share of 32.2%—while boating dropped from 19.0% to 10.7% (16 deaths).

Finally, the demographic contrast between who patrols the beach and who drowns is profound. Active patrolling members across SLSA clubs are approaching gender parity (59.7% male, 40.2% female). Yet 129 of the 150 people who drowned were men (86.0%) versus 21 women (14.0%)—a male mortality rate of 0.97 per 100,000 compared with 0.15 for women (6.5 times higher).

Data sources, cartographic methodology & authorship

Primary datasets (combined from 3 official sources)

  • Source 1 — Surf Life Saving Australia (2024): National Coastal Safety Report 2024 (NCSR 2024) & SLSA Annual Report 2023/24 (reporting period 1 July 2023 – 30 June 2024, plus 2014–24 ten-year series). Provides national and state drowning counts, rates, activity/location breakdowns, rescues (8,857), preventative actions (2,482,013), volunteer patrol hours (1,462,533), patrolling members (45,236), and 315 club records. surflifesaving.com.au
  • Source 2 — Australian Bureau of Statistics (2024): National, State and Territory Population (June 2024). Used to standardise state and gender mortality counts into per-capita rates per 100,000 residents. abs.gov.au
  • Source 3 — ABS / Geoscience Australia Spatial Geography: Simplified Australian State and Territory digital boundaries (australian-states GeoJSON) and Geoscience Australia coastline length statistics (used to derive club density per 1,000 km of coastline).

Cartographic design & authorship metadata

  • Map Projection: All three map idioms use EPSG:3577 (GDA94 / Australian Albers Equal-Area Conic) with standard parallels at 18°S and 36°S and central meridian at 132°E. Preserving true relative area is essential for continental choropleth and dot/symbol comparisons; Web Mercator is intentionally avoided.
  • Custom Non-Contiguous Cartogram: Computed in Python (`scripts/build_geometry.py`) by projecting state polygons into EPSG:3577, computing each state’s true Albers area, and scaling vertices around each state’s centroid so that drawn polygon area is strictly proportional to active patrolling members.
