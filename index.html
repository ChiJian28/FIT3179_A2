By Lim Chi Jian · Published October 2026 · Designed for a general Australian audience

Between the Red and Yellow Flags

Across 315 Surf Life Saving clubs, Australian volunteer lifesavers and lifeguards intervened 2,482,013 times in 2023/24 to stop beachgoers from entering hazardous surf—keeping drowning deaths between the patrolled red and yellow flags at zero.

Yet outside those patrolled flags and patrol hours, Australia recorded 150 coastal drowning deaths in 2023/24—the highest toll since national records began in 2004, concentrated along rugged coastlines far from volunteer patrol services.

Section 01 · National Ten-Year Trajectory

Proactive prevention surged by 39%, cutting rescues even as unpatrolled drownings hit a 20-year high.

Over the past decade (2014/15–2023/24), Australian surf life saving services transformed coastal protection. Patrolling members stepped up preventative actions—warning swimmers and guiding beachgoers back between the flags—from 1.20 million in 2014/15 to 2,482,013 in 2023/24 (+39% above the ten-year mean), while emergency in-water rescues fell 13% to 8,857.

Yet coastal drowning deaths moved in the opposite direction. Since the post-lockdown domestic coastal tourism surge of 2020/21, annual fatalities have stayed above the ten-year average of 128 deaths per year, peaking at 150 deaths in 2023/24. Every fatal incident happened at an unpatrolled location, outside patrol hours, or outside the flags.

Section 02 · Geographic Risk & Per-Capita Standardisation

New South Wales recorded the highest raw toll, but Tasmania suffered triple the national drowning rate.

Mapping raw death counts across states would mislead readers by reflecting population size: New South Wales (population 8.36 million) recorded 61 coastal deaths, whereas island Tasmania (0.57 million) recorded 10 deaths. Standardising by ABS resident population reveals true per-capita risk: Tasmania’s rate of 1.74 per 100,000 people is triple the 0.56 national benchmark.

The year-on-year slope chart explains why smaller jurisdictions experience steeper rate swings: Tasmania’s toll rose from 3 deaths in 2022/23 to 10 in 2023/24, driving its rate from 0.52 to 1.74. Meanwhile, Queensland reduced its coastal toll from 27 to 23 deaths (0.49 to 0.42 per 100,000), remaining well below the national rate alongside Victoria (0.38).

Section 03 · Patrol Capacity, Cartogram Distortion & Remoteness

Volunteer patrols cluster along the east coast, while 69% of deaths happen more than a kilometre away.

Combining SLSA patrol records with ABS and Geoscience Australia coastline data highlights a stark geographic divide. Over 71% of Australia’s 1,462,533 volunteer patrol hours and 45,236 patrolling members sit in New South Wales (685,090 hours; 129 clubs) and Queensland (356,662 hours; 57 clubs). In the cartogram below, eastern states dominate while Tasmania (792 patrollers) shrinks.

Because surf life saving clubs can guard only a fraction of Australia’s coastline, 104 of the 150 coastal drowning deaths (69%) occurred more than 1 km from the nearest service. Across ten seasons of state mortality rates (heatmap below), Tasmania and Western Australia consistently record darker high-risk cells where rugged, unpatrolled coastlines stretch for thousands of kilometres.

Section 04 · Locations, Activities & Demographic Disparity

Beaches, swimming, and men account for the overwhelming majority of coastal drowning deaths.

Over half of all coastal drowning deaths occurred at a beach (56.0%, 84 of 150), ahead of offshore waters (18.7%, 28), rocky cliffs (12.7%, 19), and other coastal waters (12.7%, 19). Among beach deaths, 34.5% (29) involved a confirmed rip and 41.7% (35) had unknown rip status, while swimming and wading reached 40.7%.

Finally, the gender contrast between who patrols the beach and who drowns is stark. Active patrolling members across SLSA clubs are near gender parity (59.7% male, 40.2% female). Yet 129 of the 150 people who drowned were men (86.0%) versus 21 women (14.0%)—a male mortality rate of 0.97 per 100,000 compared with 0.15 for women (6.5 times higher).

Data sources, cartographic methodology & authorship

Primary datasets (combined from 3 official sources)

  • Source 1 — Surf Life Saving Australia (2024): National Coastal Safety Report 2024 (NCSR 2024) & SLSA Annual Report 2023/24 (1 July 2023 – 30 June 2024, plus 2014–24 ten-year series). Provides national and state drowning counts, rates, activity/location breakdowns, rescues (8,857), preventative actions (2,482,013), volunteer patrol hours (1,462,533), patrolling members (45,236), and 315 club records. surflifesaving.com.au
  • Source 2 — Australian Bureau of Statistics (2024): National, State and Territory Population (June 2024). Used to standardise state and gender mortality counts into per-capita rates per 100,000 residents. abs.gov.au
  • Source 3 — ABS / Geoscience Australia Spatial Geography: Simplified Australian State and Territory digital boundaries (australian-states GeoJSON) and Geoscience Australia coastline length statistics (used to derive club density per 1,000 km of coastline).

Cartographic design & authorship metadata

  • Map Projection: All three map idioms use EPSG:3577 (GDA94 / Australian Albers Equal-Area Conic) with standard parallels at 18°S and 36°S and central meridian at 132°E. Preserving true relative area is essential for continental choropleth and symbol comparisons; Web Mercator is intentionally avoided.
  • Custom Non-Contiguous Cartogram: Computed in Python (`scripts/build_geometry.py`) by projecting state polygons into EPSG:3577, computing each state’s true Albers area, and scaling vertices around each state’s centroid so that drawn polygon area is strictly proportional to active patrolling members.
